Post-mortems of brands that died, and of a few that won’t stay dead

Each Brand Obituary reconstructs one company’s life and death from its SEC filings, court records, contemporary press and archived web pages: the market it was born into, the unit economics, the money, the people, the map, and the decisions that ended it.

Two tiers

Dead

The company and its business are gone. Assets were sold or liquidated, and nothing trades under the name.

Ghost

The company failed, but the name was sold and lives on, usually attached to a different, smaller business than the one that made it famous.

How these are made

  1. Every figure links to a numbered source. SEC filings are cited by accession number.
  2. Numbers we calculate are labeled as derived, and claims we couldn’t verify are labeled too.
  3. Old websites are shown as live views of Internet Archive captures, not mock-ups.
  4. Homage sections are original recreations of a brand’s look, never its logo.

Dead

Pets.com

Feb 1999 – Nov 2000
Dot-coms

The sock puppet, a Super Bowl ad and Amazon as its biggest shareholder. It sold most of its goods for less than they cost to buy and ship, and lasted 270 days as a public company.

No. 002
Dead

Webvan

Dec 1996 – Jul 2001
Dot-coms

Online groceries delivered inside a 30-minute window. It built automated warehouses for 8,000 orders a day, merged with its closest rival, and shut down in one day.

$34 → $0.06
WBVN share price, first-quarter high in 1999 to the last reported price in July 2001
No. 003
Ghost

RadioShack

1921 – 2015, and after
Consumer products

The parts store within a short drive of nearly every American. It kept 4,300 stores while its business moved to phones and online, went bankrupt twice, and its name now belongs to a retailer in El Salvador.

5,289 → 4,216
company-operated U.S. stores, from the 2001 peak to November 2014
